Tankless Water Heater Size in Bell, CA: Sizing Method

Size a tankless heater with two numbers. The first is flow rate, the gallons per minute of every hot water fixture you expect to run at the same moment. The second is temperature rise, which is the output temperature you want minus the temperature of the water coming in.

A unit must deliver your flow at your rise. Pick one that cannot, and the water runs lukewarm exactly when the house is busiest. The steps below follow the method in the Energy.gov sizing guide.

The sizing method in four steps

Tankless heaters are rated by the temperature rise they can deliver at a given flow. Work through these in order.

  1. 1

    List what runs together

    Decide which hot water devices could be on at once, such as a shower and a kitchen faucet, or two showers and a dishwasher.

  2. 2

    Add their flow rates

    Each fixture lists gallons per minute on its label or in its manual. Add up the ones you listed. This is your peak flow.

  3. 3

    Find the temperature rise

    Subtract the temperature of your incoming water from the temperature you want at the tap. Cold incoming water means a larger rise.

  4. 4

    Match to a unit

    Compare your flow and rise with the manufacturer's performance chart. The guide's minimum for a whole-house demand-type unit is a flow rate of at least 3.25 gallons per minute.

Why the numbers shift from home to home

Two houses with the same unit can get different results. Colder incoming water raises the temperature rise, which lowers the flow a unit can deliver. A family that showers at once needs a higher peak flow than one that staggers use.

Low-flow fixtures help, since they shrink the peak number. Before you buy, measure or look up the real ratings on your fixtures rather than guessing. Use your own incoming temperature, which a plumber can measure or help you estimate.

Hard water matters for a tankless in Bell

Golden State Water's Bell-Bell Gardens report for 2025 shows hardness of 137 to 280 mg/L, averaging 210 mg/L. The USGS scale calls anything over 180 very hard. A tankless heater has narrow passages inside its heat exchanger, and scale narrows them further.

The practical tie-back is a maintenance plan. Ask the maker how often to flush the unit and whether water treatment is recommended for your supply. The figures apply to Golden State Water addresses only, so check your bill for your provider.

Checks beyond the heater size

A correctly sized unit still fails if the house cannot support it.

  • Gas line size and capacity, since tankless units draw gas at a high rate
  • Venting route and clearance to windows and walls
  • Electrical outlet for the controls and fan
  • Location and access for service and flushing
  • Condensate drain if the unit is a condensing model
  • Existing recirculation or hot water loop arrangements

Gas and venting are not DIY

Gas lines and exhaust vents are safety systems. A line that is too small starves the burner, and a bad vent can spill exhaust into the home. If you smell gas, leave the building and call 911 and the gas utility from outside. Sizing is something you can learn. Gas and vent work belongs to licensed plumbers.

Reading a manufacturer performance chart

Most makers publish a table that lists the flow a unit delivers at several temperature rise values. Find the row for your rise, then read across to the flow. If that flow is below your peak, the unit is too small for your plan.

Round toward a larger unit only if the gas line and vent can support it. A bigger unit needs more gas, so sizing up on paper can create a problem in the wall. Ask the plumber to confirm both numbers together.

Tank or tankless, a fair comparison

Storage heaters are rated by first hour rating, the gallons of hot water the unit can supply in an hour. You choose one whose rating at least matches your busiest hour. Tankless units skip the storage idea, so the question becomes whether it can keep up with your peak flow at the right temperature.

Neither option is better for every home. Tankless frees floor space, while storage suits homes that cannot upgrade the gas line or vent. See tankless water heater work and water heater installation for what each involves.

Permits and rental homes

If your water has scale problems

Where scale is heavy, water treatment can protect a tankless heat exchanger. Not all treatment is equal, so ask what each option does and does not change. See water softener and filtration for the options a plumber can discuss.
