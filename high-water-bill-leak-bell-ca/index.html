High Water Bill in Bell, CA: How to Find a Leak

A jump in your water bill with no change in how many people live in the home usually points to a leak. The EPA says a family of four using more than 12,000 gallons a month in winter may have serious leaks. Two quick tests, the meter check and the toilet dye test, find most of them without any tools.

Do the tests below in order. If they show a leak, you will know whether to call the water company about the meter or a plumber about the pipes.

Find the leak in four moves

These steps follow the EPA WaterSense Fix a Leak guidance and need only a clock and some food coloring.

  1. 1

    Compare to your normal

    Look at the last few bills. If usage rose with no change in who lives there, keep going. If a relative moved in, the rise may be real use.

  2. 2

    Run the meter check

    Turn off every tap and appliance, read the meter, wait two hours with no water use and read it again. Any change suggests a leak.

  3. 3

    Do the toilet dye test

    Drop food coloring in each tank and wait ten minutes without flushing. Color in the bowl means a leaking flapper or fill valve.

  4. 4

    Look for the obvious drips

    Check faucets, under sinks, the water heater, hose bibs and any irrigation. Make a note of any damp patch in a yard near the meter.

Use the shutoff to split the problem in two

Close the main valve to the house and watch the meter. If the dial stops, the leak is inside the home. If it keeps moving with the main closed, the leak sits between the meter and your shutoff, so tell the water company and a plumber.

Bigger households are not always leaks

Census numbers show Bell averages 3.49 persons per household, and the figure in nearby Cudahy is 3.80. More people means more showers, loads of laundry and toilet flushes. A bill that rises after a new baby or a visiting relative may be an honest reflection of use.

The EPA 12,000 gallon mark is for a family of four in winter, when outdoor use is low. If your household is larger, scale that expectation up and use the meter test as the final judge, since it ignores how many people live there.

Leaks that do not show on the floor

Some of the costliest leaks never leave a puddle. Water can seep into soil beneath a slab or from a buried line. The meter keeps moving, the bill keeps rising and nothing looks wrong at the surface.

Pinpointing the spot is a job for listening and heat-sensing equipment. Our page on leak detection explains how the visit works. The same tracing work is done for homes in Cudahy and Maywood, where dense housing and shared lines can make the source harder to spot.

What renters should do with a high bill

Many Bell households rent, so the first call is often to the landlord, not a plumber.

  • Tell the landlord or property manager in writing and attach the meter reading
  • Take dated photos of any damp spots, stains or dripping fixtures
  • Ask who pays the water bill, since in many rentals it is included in rent
  • Do not hire a plumber to open walls without the landlord's approval
  • Keep a copy of every message and the bills for later disputes
  • Check renter's insurance for personal property if water has reached your belongings

Reading the meter without guessing

Meters usually sit in a box near the curb or the property line. Lift the lid carefully and watch for insects or debris. The register shows a number that climbs as water passes, and many meters also have a small triangle or wheel that spins with even a slow flow.

If you cannot find the box or the lid is stuck, ask your water company rather than prying it open. Take a clear photo of the reading each time so you are comparing the same digits. A photo with a time stamp also helps if you later need to explain the test to a landlord or the company.

Fixing it and getting the next bill checked

Once a leak is repaired, wait for the next billing cycle and compare use to your normal. If the number has not come down, the first leak may not have been the only one. Run the meter check again after the repair.

Insurance may respond to sudden accidental damage but typically not to a worn part or a slow leak. Read your policy or ask the insurer before work starts. A plumber's written invoice and the meter readings are the documents most useful to a claim.
