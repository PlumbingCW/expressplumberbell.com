Toilet overflowing or leaking onto the floor

Water on the bathroom floor usually comes from one of three places. The bowl is clogged and rising, the tank or its supply line is leaking, or the seal under the toilet has failed. Each one calls for a different fix, so the first job is working out which it is.

If the bowl is rising, do not flush again. Lift the tank lid and push the flapper down into its seat, or close the wall valve, so no more water arrives. Then keep towels at the base and move anything valuable off the floor.

If water seeps from the base only after a flush, the wax ring or the closet flange is the likely cause. That leak is slow and easy to ignore, but it soaks subfloor material from below. Tell the plumber when you first noticed the dampness.

While you wait for the plumber

These steps are safe for most homeowners and tenants, and they limit the damage before the visit.

  • Close the valve on the wall behind the toilet by turning it clockwise
  • Do not flush or run the tub and sink if the bowl is full and rising
  • Lay towels around the base and wipe standing water so nobody slips
  • Take photos of the damp floor and walls for your insurer or landlord
  • Keep children and pets out of the bathroom if sewage came up
  • Never mix drain chemicals, because they can splash back and burn skin

Why a quiet running toilet wastes so much water

A flapper that barely leaks makes almost no sound, so the loss goes unnoticed for months. The EPA WaterSense program notes that a worn flapper can silently leak thousands of gallons a year. The only sign may be a higher water bill.

The EPA also describes a simple dye test. Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank and wait about 10 minutes without flushing. If color shows up in the bowl, the flapper or seat is leaking, and a plumber can swap the part during one visit.

Mineral scale makes this worse. Golden State Water reports that its Bell-Bell Gardens supply averages 12 grains per gallon, which the USGS scale calls very hard. Scale can roughen the flapper seat and clog fill valve ports, so a part may wear out sooner at addresses served by that system. Your bill shows who supplies your water.

How a toilet replacement visit goes

Sometimes a cracked bowl or a repeat leak makes a new fixture the better choice. Here is the usual order of work.

  1. 1

    Shut off and drain

    The plumber closes the supply valve, flushes the tank empty and sponges out what is left in the bowl.

  2. 2

    Disconnect and lift

    The supply line comes off, the bolts are removed and the old toilet is lifted straight up and carried out.

  3. 3

    Check the flange

    The plumber inspects the closet flange for cracks or rust and repairs it if it is not solid. A bad flange will leak under any new toilet.

  4. 4

    Set the new fixture

    A fresh wax ring goes down, the bowl is seated and the bolts are tightened evenly so the porcelain does not crack.

  5. 5

    Test and review

    The tank is filled, the toilet is flushed several times and every joint is checked dry. You see the result before the plumber leaves.

Renters and rental properties in Bell

Census data shows about 75 percent of Bell housing is renter occupied, so many toilet calls start with a tenant. Tell your landlord or property manager as soon as water is on the floor, and keep the message in writing. Ask who approves the work before the plumber begins.

If the toilet is backing up and a tub or sink nearby is gurgling too, the problem may be further down the line. In that case drain cleaning or a look at the main line is the better next step. A slow leak that keeps returning may deserve leak detection to find where the water is actually going.

Permits, licensing and who does the work

A straight toilet swap in the same spot rarely needs more than a careful installer. If the job moves the drain or changes piping, permit rules vary, so ask the Bell Building and Safety Division at City Hall, 6330 Pine Ave. Toilet installation falls under the California Plumbing Code, which is adopted statewide and based on the UPC.
